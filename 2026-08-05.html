The challenge of platform capture
Emily Bell · Columbia Journalism Review · 2019

Bell's insight was to locate media capture not in ownership or regulation but in infrastructure: once platforms became the layer that decides who reaches an audience, publishers were captured by the economics of distribution itself. She saw that dependency on someone else's pipes is a governance question, not just a business one. Five years on, the terms of that dependency have only hardened, and the publishers who leaned in have the least leverage to renegotiate.

Months before Substack switched on its Pangram detector, Taylor Lorenz ran the same tool across the platform's top content. Tech newsletters topping the AI-generated charts surprised nobody. The harder finding sat lower: philosophy and health writing clocked in at 22 to 23 percent machine-generated (categories a reader subscribes to precisely because a trusted human authority is supposed to be home). Sources flagged the breakdown in April and drew no hard conclusions. The numbers have been waiting for a fight to belong to.

LinkedIn and X Are Flooded With AI Spam, Browsing Data Suggests

Jason Koebler's data moves the question from how much AI text exists to how much readers actually meet. The numbers come from Pangram scanning passively as people browse (a measure of the feed as lived, not a theoretical platform-wide total). LinkedIn is the flood zone, with 41 percent of the longform users encounter likely machine-generated; X runs close to a third; Reddit and Substack land near one in ten.

Zinstrel - AI Music Culture & Analysis

When AI Transparency Becomes a Penalty

Crediting a collaborator is generosity; a label that kills your reach is a fine. Zinstrel argues the platforms keep blurring the two, with transparency language laundering what functions as punishment, and though the piece is written about music streaming, it describes any service where disclosure triggers consequences. Once honest disclosure becomes the costliest choice on offer, nobody should be surprised when the tags disappear and the work keeps shipping.

The New York Times

Labels for Music Created With A.I. Could Join Explicit Lyrics Warnings

Ben Sisario covers the music industry's version of the label fight. An RIAA-led proposal would attach two marks, one for fully generated tracks and a softer variant for human work made with AI assistance, on the model of the Parental Advisory sticker. Sisario grounds the stakes at Deezer, where 75,000 AI tracks arrive every day, nearly half of everything the service ingests. Enforcement across the streaming services is the question the proposal leaves open.

Flashlight

The Stamp Got Teeth Yesterday

TIDAL was the most lucrative platform for synthetic music, paying three to five times Spotify's per-stream rate. As of mid-July it is the least: fully machine-generated tracks now earn zero royalties there, and listeners can filter them out of the service entirely. Flashlight reads the move as the moment the disclosure mark started setting the price. Whatever Substack's detector is today, TIDAL is the demonstration that a label rarely stays descriptive once money is attached.

A Different Lens

The Blues: LOCKSS it up!

The last word goes to the archive. A Different Lens splits AI blues fraud into two kinds: the fantasy fake, which announces its own fiction, and the archive fake, which impersonates a real 1962 John Lee Hooker release with no credits and no history to check. The second kind does not need to fool a listener; it needs to fool the algorithm, and the person who finds it a decade from now. Labels, stickers, and scanners are all guarding the same thing, the record of who actually made what.
