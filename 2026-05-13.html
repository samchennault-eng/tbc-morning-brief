404 Media · AI + Content & Editorial Technology
Your AI Use Is Breaking My Brain
Jason Koebler at 404 Media articulates something many of us have felt but struggled to name: the creeping epistemic exhaustion of not knowing whether the text in front of you was written by a person or assembled by a model. The piece isn't about AI slop in the abstract — it's about what happens to your reading brain when you can no longer trust the basic contract that someone meant what they wrote. Koebler traces how this uncertainty bleeds into everything: emails that feel off, comments that seem too polished, the slow erosion of the assumption that communication implies intent. For anyone building or editing in this environment, it's a necessary articulation of the cognitive tax we're all now paying.

Jazz Notes · Music & Scene
Dinner at the Mangiones'
Jazz Notes uses Chuck Mangione's death to surface what the obituaries missed: that his lone Art Blakey album also marks the final recording of Frank Mitchell, a tenor saxophonist murdered before he turned thirty. Blakey told critics to watch him. Jarrett shared the stage. None of it mattered — Mitchell vanished from the narrative anyway. The piece is quietly devastating for how it uses one musician's erasure to say something true about jazz historiography: the way the canon gets written around stars, over everyone else, until whole lives disappear into sideman credits and footnotes.

Call & Response with Michelle Mercer · Voice & Culture
Against the List, for Love of Art
Call & Response mounts a defense of critical judgment against the tyranny of the ranked list — not as aesthetic snobbery, but as a structural argument about what we lose when every cultural conversation gets flattened into ordinal positions. The piece is useful precisely because it doesn't just complain about listicles; it articulates what lists optimize for (shareability, argument-settling, algorithmic legibility) and what they sacrifice (the slow accretion of taste, the permission to love something without defending its rank). Anyone who's felt the pressure to reduce criticism to rankings will recognize the exhaustion here.

02 Music & Scene Music
First Floor
Eurovision Is More Radical Than the Biennale
First Floor makes a pointed structural argument: Eurovision, a pop contest with a no-politics rule, has done more to exclude state actors accused of atrocities than the Venice Biennale, a prestige art institution that theoretically stands for something. The comparison lands — five countries boycotted Eurovision over Israel's inclusion, while the Biennale's entire jury resigned rather than award prizes, yet the art world event largely proceeded anyway. The piece is useful for how clearly it exposes the gap between cultural institutions' self-image and their actual leverage.

03 Music Industry Analysis Music
Music Business Worldwide
Tencent Music's 'Industry Chaos' Caused by AI-Generated Content on Streaming Platforms
Music Business Worldwide reports that what Western labels are treating as a policy problem, Tencent Music is living through as a business crisis. On their Q1 earnings call, Tencent's leadership described 'industry chaos' from AI-generated content flooding rival platforms — specifically 'song-washing,' mass uploads of AI-modified copyrighted tracks used to pad libraries and attract subscribers. CEO Ross Liang named it as a direct cause of Tencent's subscriber revenue decline. A useful data point for anyone who thinks AI music flooding is a hypothetical.

Rick Beato Versus the NY Times
Institutional credibility is losing to personal authority in real time — and a viral pile-on involving Rick Beato and a New York Times songwriter ranking is the latest evidence. On Substack, the argument is that Beato's rebuttal video, already pulling ten times the views of the original Times piece, illustrates a structural shift: a trusted individual with genuine musical credentials outpunching a legacy outlet whose critics, in Brad Mehldau's framing, listen to music for self-definition rather than love of the form. (What's the Story) Music Glory?🦎
Who Gives A F*** About Stock Music? Apparently Everyone.
Stock music didn't win on quality — it won on friction. That's the core argument in this Substack piece, which opens with a number worth sitting with: the stock music industry is worth $1.3 billion and climbing toward $2.8 billion by 2030, while global sync revenue for the entire recorded music catalog came in around $650 million last year. The piece frames this gap not as a market quirk but as a structural wealth transfer away from actual artists — one that branded social media licensing, still built around slow human negotiation, keeps accelerating.

STVDIO
Suno: and why the music industry is built by those that "ask forgiveness"
STVDIO makes the case that Suno's rise isn't an anomaly — it's the industry's oldest pattern playing out again. Napster, YouTube, TikTok: the platforms that moved without permission built the market; the ones that waited for clearance mostly didn't survive. Suno is now valued at $5 billion, has 100 million users, and generates 7 million songs a day, all while holding almost no licenses beyond a single Warner settlement. The piece is useful for naming the structural incentive clearly: in music, forgiveness has historically been cheaper than permission.

Montreal AI Ethics Institute
Collected for One Thing, Used for Another: Therapy sessions, Slack archives, and the data that outlives its original purpose
The Montreal AI Ethics Institute's latest brief makes a structural argument worth sitting with: data doesn't disappear when its original purpose ends — it migrates, and the relationships that gave it meaning don't travel with it. A nurse's therapy sessions became courtroom evidence. A defunct startup's Slack archive became training data. The throughline isn't misuse exactly; it's that the original consent context stops mattering entirely. For anyone building with user data — playlists, listening habits, behavioral signals — that's the uncomfortable frame.

The New York Review of Books
What Happened in Vegas
Charlie Lee's piece in *The New York Review of Books* makes a provocation worth sitting with: no good writer, living or dead, has talked about money with as much relish and zero apology as John Gregory Dunne. The occasion is Dunne's *Vegas: A Memoir of a Dark Season*, recently reprinted, and Lee uses it to argue that Dunne's particular gift — high taste in service of gutter instincts — made Las Vegas the ideal subject. The piece is a case for a kind of writer we don't really produce anymore.

Katherine Needleman Oboist's Substack
When the Crazy Stuff: Classical Music and Dead Women Composers
Katherine Needleman's piece makes an uncomfortable argument most classical institutions would prefer to ignore: the field doesn't dismiss women composers out of ignorance — it does so by design, then performs progress by spotlighting living women in brochures while the canon stays intact. Writing around her preparation of Kaija Saariaho's *Winter Sky*, Needleman is sharp on the gap between representation as optics and representation as repertoire. Worth reading for anyone thinking about how streaming platforms and labels perform inclusion without restructuring what actually gets surfaced.

The Believer Magazine
My Father's Shadow
The Believer Magazine runs a personal essay that uses one family's story to trace how conspiratorial thinking takes root — and why it's so hard to dislodge. The piece leans on Naomi Klein's Doppelganger for its sharpest insight: conspiracy theories spread because they're not entirely wrong. There's always a real betrayal at the center — the WMDs that weren't there, the recounts that didn't hold — and that kernel of truth is exactly what makes the surrounding distortion so sticky.
