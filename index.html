How to break free of Spotify's algorithm
Jennifer Kingson · MIT Technology Review · 2024

Kingson maps the specific design choices that keep listeners inside Spotify's recommendation loop: autoplay defaults, homepage weighting, playlist architecture engineered so that opting out takes deliberate effort most people never make. The value here is naming the mechanisms rather than the vibe. Discovery isn't passively shaped by an algorithm; it's actively funneled by UX decisions that treat your own curiosity as friction to be designed away.

06 Attack of the Bots 9 pieces

Much of the attention around AI in music has focused on how it fundamentally alters the artistic process, but the more immediate threat may be how it enables pre-existing bad actors and its disruptive impact on scalping in particular. In October, bots swamped RAYE's presale registration, crashed Sam Fender's presale, and disrupted a Garth Brooks on-sale. Ticketmaster UK says it blocks 200 million bot attempts a day. The US is quickly establishing a regulatory frame, albeit imperfectly. Ticket sales are now a contest between software and fans, and the people trying to referee it include the company accused of profiting from it.

Billboard

‘Supercharged’ AI Bots Are Disrupting the Ticketing Market. The Industry Is Desperate for a Solution

The UK is facing "a supercharged version coming in with AI" of the scalping the US has dealt with for a decade, one artist manager tells Billboard U.K. On one tour date by an artist the manager represents, bots bought "15% to 20%" of the tickets, many of which landed on resale sites. Canceling them just hands the same bots another chance. Labour promised a face-value resale cap in its 2024 manifesto. Thomas Smith reports that the legislation is still in draft and left out of the parliamentary session that runs until May 2027. Ticketmaster capped resale on its own site at face value in 2018, but Sarah Slater, who runs Ticketmaster U.K., says "touts can still make huge profits on other sites." The manager has little to offer clients: "how do you explain the unexplainable and tell them that there's no solution?"

08 Streaming's Home Field 9 pieces

According to early champions, streaming was supposed to flatten the globe, bringing faraway sounds into the speakers and headphones of global audiences. This actually happened, as evidenced by the explosion of Afrobeats and Latin music over the past decade. But there’s a flip side to this: increasingly, it’s diminishing the hometown audiences for acts in certain countries. Australian music is down to 8.5% of streams in Australia, with a campaign (Ausify) to win it back. Quebec's physical sales are falling, but its streaming share holds. The articles below unpack this phenomenon.

Billboard

Ausify Campaign Returns as Australian Music’s Streaming Share Hits New Low

Australians say they love their own music. In a Music Australia survey of 1,018 people, 84% said a defining life moment brings an Australian song to mind, yet only 27% had streamed an Australian artist in the past 12 months. Ausify, the campaign Music Australia runs to close that gap, treats it as a discovery problem rather than a demand problem. Billboard's Jessica Lynch lays out how steep the slide has been: local music's streaming share is down 37.5% in five years, and APRA AMCOS CEO Dean Ormston has warned it could fall to about 5% within five years without intervention. In its second year, Ausify is calling for "Public Displays of Ausify," starting with seven artists singing a karaoke "Love Is in the Air." Offshore the picture flips, with APRA's international revenue topping $100 million for the first time in FY26.

Music Business Worldwide

Australian local music is in decline, and UK, Canada aren’t far behind, new report warns

Will Page and Morgan Harrington's report for The Australia Institute points to Denmark, a country of just 5.5 million Danish speakers where Danish artists singing in Danish held 16 of the top 20 albums in the year the report measured. Australia has no such shield. The authors blame recommendation algorithms that favor the listener's own language, which in English means music from the US. Daniel Tencer's write-up at Music Business Worldwide puts a number on the cost: Australian recorded music revenue grew nearly 28% between 2021 and 2024 while local acts' revenue fell from $50.9 million to $44.8 million. The UK and Canada show milder versions of the same problem. Spotify pushes back with its own survey, in which 81% of streaming users "say it's easy to find Australian artists."

Quebec Experiences Decrease in Local Content Consumption

Only one Quebec act, Les Cowboys Fringants, makes the list of the 20 most-streamed artists in Quebec, ranking 12th. Charlotte Cardin, the next Quebecer, ranks 38th overall. Ashley King at Digital Music News files Quebec beside Australia as another market losing ground at home. The sharpest losses are in album sales, where Quebec artists' share fell from 24% to 18% in one year. Vinyl, which is growing, does little for Quebec artists (8% of sales).

Finnish music rivals pop and rock listening at home, finds report

Finnish-language pop is as popular in Finland as rock and English-language pop, according to the Music Listening in Finland report covered by IQ Magazine: the three tie as the country's most liked genres, each with 53% of the population. U.K. Music Thrives Abroad and at Home Despite Growing Global Competition

Luminate reads the UK more kindly than the report in Daniel Tencer's piece, which had British music in a streaming-era drought. UK artists drew 7.8% of global on-demand audio streams in the first half of 2026, slightly up on a year earlier, and the UK ranks as the second-largest export country in Luminate's Export Power Rankings. Luminate argues that support at home shapes how a country does abroad. At home, UK listening is up: 79% of UK respondents call themselves regular music listeners, up from 76% in 2025, and Gen Z puts in 15.2 hours a week.

Billboard

Billboard Japan & Luminate’s ‘NOW PLAYING JAPAN Vol. 6’ Focuses on Overseas Perspectives on the Japanese Market, How to Leverage Metadata

Japanese artists' share of streaming in Japan has risen to 75.2%, up from 72.8% in the first half of 2024, a long way from the Australian figures in Jessica Lynch's piece. At the NOW PLAYING JAPAN Vol. 6 conference in Tokyo, Luminate CEO Rob Jonas called that a sign of fan loyalty and said the biggest opportunity for growth lies outside the country. Streaming leads discovery abroad, but more than half of J-pop fans in Mexico say they find music through anime. Jonas's advice was to let the home market fund the push overseas. "Japan has the scale at home and the fans abroad," he said. Naohiro Kaji of the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry followed with the government's target for music's overseas sales, from 0.1 trillion yen to 0.7 trillion yen by 2033.

As Bollywood Expands Global Reach, Other Genres Gain in India

Bollywood took 33.5% of all on-demand streams in India in the first half of 2026, about twice the share of pop, the runner-up. Bollywood is growing faster abroad, though: from H1 2025 to H1 2026, its streams outside India grew 8.2% against 5.1% at home. Luminate floats the idea that it may be on the road Latin and K-pop traveled, where wider streaming adoption carried a regional sound abroad. Inside India, younger listeners lead the turn to pop, EDM and Latin, Luminate's survey suggests, and artist-driven scenes have emerged independent of the Bollywood system.

Luminate

Country Music’s Global Boom Is Another “Glocalization” Wave

Ella Langley's record-breaking 2026 run atop the Billboard Hot 100 with "Choosin' Texas" makes country look like a U.S. story. Luminate counts 185 billion on-demand streams for the genre worldwide (folk and bluegrass included) through week 41 of 2026, up 54% from the same stretch of 2021. The trigger was the pandemic: with stores shut, U.S. country fans, historically averse to streaming, switched over. Luminate calls what followed glocalization. The more U.S. country artists were streamed at home, the more visible they became to listeners abroad, and in the U.K. country listening rose more than 40% year over year in both 2023 and 2024. The genre grows its own locals: Norwegian artist Tobias Sten, rooted in classic Nashville-style country, is the most-streamed country artist in Norway.

Hypebot

Culture Shock: What 10,500 Labels Reveal About the Global South's Genre Story

MIDiA Research mapped 10,500 record labels by country, revenue, genre and distributor, and found Global South genres, from amapiano and Arabic pop to sertanejo and cumbia, already holding around a fifth of revenue, streams and label count. In a preview on Hypebot, MIDiA explains that revenue lags because Global South markets pay less per user. The path it expects is reggaeton's: genres build enough stream scale at home to hit global charts, the recommendation algorithms push them wider, and diaspora listeners carry them into high-paying Western markets. Labels in sub-Saharan Africa and MENA export five times less of their music than US and European labels. MIDiA's answer for Western labels, which it expects to face more competition in their own core regions, is distribution. Believe already handles around half of all Turkish labels in the top 10,500.
