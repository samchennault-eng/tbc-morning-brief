Uncanny Valley
Anna Wiener · n+1 / MCD Books · 2016

Wiener went inside the Valley as a book-publishing refugee turned data-analytics hire, and her memoir catches something most tech reporting misses: the texture of true belief. The engineers aren't villains; they're people convinced that disruption is a moral act, fluent in a language that dissolves labor into "users" and "growth." She documents how the people building the systems narrate themselves out of responsibility for what the systems do.

06 Everybody's Got a Position 8 pieces

Prediction markets have arrived in the music world. As a betting market, Kalshi's music contracts went from ~$70 million in 2025 to over $400 million this year, with most bets placed on streaming numbers for Drake, Taylor Swift, and Bad Bunny. As advertisers, the same markets paid rappers (YG, Offset, 42 Dugg) to post undisclosed midterm-bet ads. Not everyone loves this. Since June, the platforms have begun policing the influencers, and senators have pressed them about it. Music is now both something people bet on, and a way to sell betting, and the artists' cut isn't settled.

Pitchfork

Why Were So Many Rappers Betting on the Midterms?

The post was one of a run of undisclosed Kalshi ads from rappers who peaked in the 2010s and early 2020s. Pitchfork's Alphonse Pierre sees them as one more sign of the internet becoming "a land of over-the-hill artists feeding the algorithm and selling out for checks." Pierre wants to know what billion-dollar corporations want with past-their-prime regional rappers. Part of the answer is language. In a Wired interview, Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour insisted the word "gambling" was wrong for the company, which he considers a "financial market." Rappers, Pierre writes, can make a word stick in people's heads, which is exactly what these companies want. Dugg's pick won. X took down a few of the posts for breaking its rules.

The prediction market to church pipeline

On a prediction market, the Digital Culturist newsletter writes, "the bet becomes a position." Its working theory is that a few trends hitting young men are really one funnel. Red-pill content prices manhood in money, betting apps sell the fastest route to it, prediction markets dress the habit up as financial savvy, and conservative religion catches the fall with absolution that asks for no reckoning. 08 The Residency Turn 12 pieces

Big tours are turning into residencies, with artists playing one city for many nights, and fans traveling for it. This moves the cost from artist to fan and turns concerts into tourism. Harry Styles's 30-night run at the Garden set the template, with 11.5 million registrations for about 540,000 seats; Oasis made the world tour look like bad business; Luminate found a lasting streaming lift from the Sphere; and hotel chains and a federal bill are building tourism around it. Now, the model has reached Latin touring (Bad Bunny's and Shakira's destination residencies). This changes who can go and what it costs to get there.

Harry Styles and the Residencies Taking Over the Music Industry

Billy Joel's Garden run took ten years and 104 sellouts. Harry Styles opened 30 nights there on Aug. 26, 2026, part of a tour of 67 dates in just seven cities. The Times lays out why the industry keeps choosing more shows in fewer places: savings on trucking and fuel, and room for bigger productions. When a residency replaces a tour, though, the financial burden moves onto ticket buyers. Jonathan Daniel of Crush Music, which manages Green Day and Alanis Morissette, concedes it is worse for fans. Craig Church, a fan from the Bay Area, showed the paper his receipts: about $450 per Wembley floor spot, inside a London trip that cost roughly $10,000.

How Bad Bunny and Shakira’s Epic Residencies Changed the Game for Latin Touring

Madrid has two major stadiums, and when Live Nation couldn't get either one for Shakira, it built its own. Estadio Shakira, a 50,000-capacity venue made for the Colombian superstar, hosted 13 shows that grossed $81.1 million. Billboard's Taylor Mims sets it beside Bad Bunny's 31 nights in San Juan in 2025, where promoter Alejandro Pabón figures around 200,000 fans traveled in, most of them from the U.S. Henry Cárdenas of Cárdenas Marketing Network has a Latin artist set to announce a 25-show residency in Mexico. When he asked Sphere about 2027 dates, he was told it was booked solidly beyond that year. "The problem is going to become the venues," he says.

Joel Gouveia's Substack

Oasis Is Making The World Tour Look Stupid

Joel Gouveia, who once ran a music festival, had to count the Oasis dates on the screen with a finger. Eleven nights at Manchester's Etihad Stadium, roughly 50,000 tickets a night, over half a million tickets in one building in under a month. Gouveia's case is that touring is a holdover from the CD era, when the concert was a moving billboard for the record in each city, and that streaming broke that logic. Oasis also collected registrants' emails and locations through its own label and holding company. Gouveia argues that's how the band knew it could book the same stadium for 11 nights. The model has a ceiling, and Gouveia says so: an indie act couldn't sell 2,000 tickets over multiple nights in one city, which is why it plays thirty cities.

Luminate

It Pays to Play the Sphere, the Vegas Venue Boosting Artist Streams

U2 opened the Sphere, the Las Vegas venue that books residency-length runs, with 40 shows across 2023 and 2024. In the run's first week, the band's U.S. on-demand audio streams jumped 46% year over year, to 11.9 million, with no tour and no new album (just the single "Atomic City"). Luminate's point is that a Sphere residency pays off past the box office, in long-term increases in listening, mostly for legacy acts with deep catalogs and a reputation for spectacle. The DJ Anyma, much smaller on streaming, saw his streams rise 154% from the 12 weeks before his first Sphere show to the 12 weeks after. Kenny Chesney and the Eagles, among others, got no meaningful boost, which Luminate speculates comes down to audiences that are less online.

IQ Magazine

Bill to boost US music tourism nears approval

The American Music Tourism Act directs the Commerce Department's National Travel and Tourism Office to promote domestic and international travel to U.S. music festivals, concert venues and other music sites, and to report to Congress within a year of enactment. By September 2026 the bipartisan bill had passed the House and awaited President Trump's approval, IQ Magazine reported, with endorsements from Live Nation, NIVA and the RIAA. IQ ties it to a series of promoter deals with hotel chains and to analysis suggesting music tourism could reach $400.5 billion by 2032. NIVA executive director Stephen Parker said independent venues and festivals draw visitors: "Fans travel for them. They book hotels, fill restaurants, and shop on Main Street because a show is happening that night."
