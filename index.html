Holly Herndon on AI and Music
Jacobin · Jacobin · 2024

Herndon built Spawn, an AI trained on her own voice, then argued the hard part isn't the technology but the ownership question underneath it: an AI model is collective intelligence, assembled from other people's work, and the only ethical response is consent and compensation at the source. Years before the current licensing scramble, she was treating training data as labor rather than raw material, and building the infrastructure to prove an artist-first model could actually work.

06 Hits, Out of Time 10 pieces

One of the dominant truisms of late-streaming is that the album release cycle is forever broken. A track or an album's fate is no longer decided at or near the release date, and sometimes it takes months or even years for a song to take hold. The pieces below look at a few different flavors of this: a dismissed Aphex Twin album that contains what are now some of his most popular songs; a 69-year-old Uber driver turned cult figure. This feels less like a repeatable model, and more like a whim of fate and circumstance, though that doesn’t stop the analysts below from making sense of it all.

Chartmetric

Forget First-Day Streams: Albums Break Out Months Later

On stan Twitter, an album's first 24 hours of Spotify streams settle whether it flopped. Andy Steiner of Chartmetric argues the better measure is growth over the following year, and that promotion after release may count for more than the rollout. Tame Impala's Deadbeat drew tepid reviews and thin playlisting for its lead single before a JENNIE remix of "Dracula" and a Spider-Man: Brand New Day sync for "Loser" lifted the whole album. Kevin Parker's project went from 35.1M Spotify monthly listeners before the album to 62.7M twelve months later. "Loser" alone grew by more than 150M streams in the month after the film hit theaters.

Ben at STVDIO

The fastest growing half of music (is also the most confusing)

STVDIO's Benjamin James follows the money behind Kate Bush's "Running Up That Hill," the revival cited in The Guardian's Cliff Richard piece. Bush rarely licenses her music, so to land the song for Stranger Things in 2022, the show's music supervisor wrote her a long, personal essay asking for clearance. After the sync fee came royalties for every view of the episode and for a 9,000% streaming bump; in all, the placement earned Bush an estimated $3.5 million in fees and downstream royalties. Publishing, the songwriting half of the business, gets paid wherever a song turns up again. The year of the sync was also, coincidentally, the first year US publishing growth overtook recorded-music growth, James notes, and it has stayed ahead every year since.

Meanwhile crate diggers in Europe and America were playing the title track, J Dilla sampled him, and his records were reissued. The Quietus's tribute is an unpublished interview Filip Kalinowski did backstage in Warsaw, when Reindorf had just turned 70 and was performing again after almost four decades, still asking the crowd if they wanted more six or seven tracks into the encore. "It's my big comeback," he said. "I'm ready to achieve what I missed out on years ago."

08 What a Ticket Buys 12 pieces

Concert tickets have always been hierarchical: those snuggled up front and center have a far different experience than those peering at the stage from the cheap seats. But status is now far more explicit, and concerts are now priced like airline tickets, with certain "packages" speaking as much to status as to fandom. These experiential tiers explain how the live boom and Blue Dot Fever can both be true. The pieces below explore what this means for the live music industry, and what it means for fans.

Billboard

Are Sky-High Concert Ticket Prices Sustainable For the Live Business?

Robert Levine's first concert was Rush, in the spring of 1986, and the ticket cost $15.50. The cheapest seat Levine could find for Rush's 2026 tour was $175, and inflation explains only part of that jump. The Billboard writer thinks the obsession with fairness is a bit silly, since fans who pay these prices must feel they're worth it. Levine worries more that people fall out of the habit. Sold at "an array of prices for a variety of kinds of status," concerts are turning from a regular night out for young people into "an occasional indulgence for superfans or the wealthy." Levine has no fix, only a modest ask that artists keep some tickets inexpensive, "so fans can at least get into the arena."
