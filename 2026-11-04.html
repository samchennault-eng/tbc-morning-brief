Rock Lyrics Are Poetry (Maybe)
Robert Christgau · Cheetah Magazine · 1967

Christgau wrote this before rock criticism existed as a discipline, and in a sense he invented the discipline by writing it. His argument isn't that rock lyrics clear the bar of poetry but that measuring them against poetry misses the point: the words work in service of the record, not the page. That move, taking a disposable commercial form seriously on its own terms rather than borrowing another medium's prestige, is the founding gesture of everything criticism has done with pop since.

06 Who Names the Genre 17 pieces

Our world is much larger these days, and we've struggled to find common words for things. In music, this expresses itself in the awkward ways that western audiences (and their platform/label gatekeepers) talk about genre. The articles below explore how terms such as "Afrobeats" or "Asian pop" create distance more than they describe specific things. As you'll see, this isn't a new phenomenon, and speaks to a broader power dynamic that influences who gets to name a sound.

The Guardian

The west has a blinkered view of African music – and it’s hurting the whole continent’s culture

When Tyla won best Afrobeats at the 2024 MTV VMAs, she used part of her speech to push back on the label. "There's a tendency to group all African music as Afrobeats," she told the audience, before naming her own sound, South Africa's amapiano. The term dates to the early 2010s, when London DJ Abrantee added an "s" to Fela Kuti's Afrobeat to market West African dance music. Chibuzo Emmanuel argues in The Guardian that the catch-all treats new West African sounds as fodder for the Afrobeats machine, hyped and then discarded. Mr Eazi's banku music, a blend of Nigerian pop and Ghanaian highlife with its own four-stroke kick-drum pattern, faded because no one marketed it to the west or framed it locally as a distinct sound. Azonto, zanku, cruise and afropiano went the same way.

Pacific Standard

What Is Musical Genre Exactly?

When their 1927 record was categorized as a race record, the Allen Brothers, a white duo from Chattanooga, sued Columbia Records for $250,000. "It would have hurt us in getting dates if people who didn't know us thought we were black," one of the brothers later explained. Jack Denton recounts the case in an April 2019 Pacific Standard piece, written after Billboard pulled Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" from its country chart. From the 1920s, the record business sorted music by who was buying it, marking race records Black and hillbilly music white and leaving pop white but unmarked. Billboard even shelved its R&B chart for 14 months in the mid-1960s because it looked too much like the pop chart. "The answer was not that the music all sounded the same," UCLA musicologist Robert Fink says. "It was that everybody seemed to be listening to the same stuff."

The National

A history of denial: the problem with the term 'urban music'

In The National, Saeed Saeed traces "urban music" back to the 1920s, writing days after Republic Records dropped the term in June 2020 as anti-racism protests swept the globe. The label behind Drake and Ariana Grande cut it from its marketing, its divisions and its job descriptions. Before "urban" came the race record, the official term until 1949, when Billboard renamed its chart Rhythm and Blues. "Urban" arrived in 1974, largely credited to Black New York DJ Frankie Crocker, who used it at WBLS for the disco, funk, R&B and early rap on his playlists. Labels took it up to sell Black artists, and it soon read as shorthand for "ghetto." For four decades, Saeed writes, those artists stayed locked in the category while white pop acts from George Michael to Ed Sheeran adopted their music.

The Arts Desk

Opinion: Time to say goodbye to the label 'World Music'

In 1987, London DJs, musicians and record people met in an Islington pub to put all the foreign music they wanted to sell under one name. fRoots editor Ian Anderson, who was there, listed the losers, among them "Worldbeat" (left out anything without drums) and "Tropical" (bye-bye Bulgarians). "World music" won on a show of hands. Howard Male once felt the label did more good than harm, but by 2011, in a column for The Arts Desk, Male had decided it had had its day. The West splits its own pop into dozens of genres, in Male's account, while the rest of the planet's sounds "have to just make do with this one essentially meaningless signpost." Would you, Male asks, "be drawn to a restaurant that just called itself World Food?"
