The White Noise Supremacists
Lester Bangs · The Village Voice · 1979

Bangs turned the critic's job inside out here, indicting the casual racism of the late-'70s punk scene and his own complicity in it. The piece refuses the comfortable distance of aesthetic judgment: it insists that what we celebrate, excuse, or let slide in a scene carries a moral weight the critic can't launder away. Decades on, it remains the sharpest argument that writing about culture is never neutral stenography.

When Markus Svensson went on the Swedish public broadcaster SVT to discuss his album as The Tarantula Waltz, he was shut down for calling out Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson for "crawling into bed with a racist party" and told to "stick to the music." He came away wondering whether SVT had grown wary of the Sweden Democrats' threats to cut its funding. In The Quietus, Alan Pedder talks to Swedish musicians ahead of the 2026 election about a scene that stayed largely apolitical while SD became the country's second-largest party in 2022. Folk musician Sara Parkman says there's been an idea that artists need "to be neutral and treat SD like a normal party, even though they aren't." Parkman sees episodes like Svensson's as symptoms of the right-wing governing bloc's turn toward private funding for culture, and asks, "Who gets to decide what art is being made?"

The Guardian

Their orchestra is not a sacred object but a tool for struggle – Paris’s musicians join the frontline

During the student protests in France, gas canisters landed in double-bass cases and L'Orchestre du Nouveau Monde kept playing. The orchestra, co-founded by conductor Étienne Jarrier and friends as teenagers, calls itself "a tool for struggle" and grades its performances on consciousness-raising impact. For Tom Service, its protests and the police's reaction to them are "a wake-up call to remind classical music where it comes from." Service places it beside the Scratch Orchestra, which Cornelius Cardew co-founded in 1969; it played the Proms in 1972 and disbanded in 1974.

08 Is Any of This Real? 12 pieces

We live in an era of unreality, where our foundations have evaporated and our ability to determine what is real and what is a product has dissolved. More so than ever before, people care about authenticity when it comes to music and culture, and also more so than ever, they’re offered music as a product and commercial construct. The pieces below look at the variety of scams and subterfuges, and search out what it means to have an authentic cultural experience.

NPR Music

Very chaotic, not at all good: Why does everything in music feel fake right now?

Session singer Yvette Marine accused Paula Abdul and her producers of using computers to build a "composite voice" on "Opposites Attract," blending Marine's guide vocal with Abdul's own. (Marine sued and lost.) Ann Powers writes that fights like that were about purity, and calling something fake was a way of saying "Stay out"; the newer ones assume fakeness has already won. Powers casts Stella Lefty as "the Paula Abdul of 2026," a TikTok star hounded by accusations that her hit "Boston" lifts the chorus of Noah Kahan's "Stick Season." Lefty's own account of writing it, "I was literally thinking about nothing at all," recalls for Powers "the serviceable obliviousness of a chatbot."

NPR Music

From ghostwriters to false identities: Our favorite fake music scandals of the 21st century

A curious Gramophone reader put a Joyce Hatto Liszt CD into his computer, and it came up as the work of the Hungarian pianist László Simon. Hatto, an English pianist of middling reputation, had released around 100 albums on her own label from the late 1980s, with her husband, William Barrington-Coupe, producing, to reviews that were close to unanimous raves. In February 2007, Barrington-Coupe admitted swapping in other pianists' performances. NPR Music's staff set the Hatto affair beside their other favorite fake-music scandals of the 21st century, among them Silibil N' Brains, two Dundee rappers laughed out of a Polydor audition as "the rapping Proclaimers" who then got signed by Sony UK as Californians. On Hatto, Tom Huizenga says the critics were not exactly duped: they really did hear "spectacular playing," just not hers.

The New Yorker

Stella Lefty and the Rise of Nepo-Baby Musicians and “Industry Plants”

Musician bio-pics nearly all run from hardship to glory, and Brady Brickner-Wood writes that "we prefer our artists broken and emotionally bedraggled." The nepo baby, in the internet's telling, skipped the heartache. Stella Lefty's "Boston" swept TikTok, and then internet sleuths discovered that her father, Eric Lefkofsky, is a Groupon co-founder turned billionaire A.I. executive. The argument since has turned less on whether he made her famous than on how lavishly he may have bankrolled her career. Brickner-Wood's larger point is that the internet promises discovery on talent alone, while the hidden variables of virality (expensive recording equipment, proximity to major cities, time to churn out content) favor anyone with a safety net. Lefty's manager, Adam Alpert, offers a flat denial: "Industry plants don't exist."

Chartmetric

How Much Does Being A Nepo Baby Help in Pop Music

Stella Lefty's father is Eric Lefkofsky, the Groupon co-founder, and her rapid rise has fed accusations that she bought her way to the top. If so, Jon O'Brien observes, she took her time about it: for several years before "Boston," her Spotify monthly listeners struggled to get past 100K, and the hit took her to 17.4 million. Where Brady Brickner-Wood weighed the resentment, O'Brien runs Chartmetric numbers on a crop of well-connected acts and finds that wealth buys playlist placements and, perhaps more importantly, time to build an audience. The most blatant case of the lot, O'Brien suggests, came when Will Ferrell played his son Magnus's single "Asleep Talking" on Michelle Obama's IMO podcast, which helped push it from 417K to 6.89M Spotify streams.

Chaotic Good: Music's TikTok Marketing

"All opinions are formed in the TikTok comments," Jesse Coren, co-founder of the marketing agency Chaotic Good, told Billboard's On The Record podcast. Once the episode aired, the outcry centered on the agency's "narrative campaign," which sends a network of accounts into the comment sections of any post about a client to drive a story about that client's talent. Harry Levin checks one client, Geese, and finds the band's TikTok posts don't draw an overwhelming number of comments next to artists of similar size; Billboard's Kristin Robinson, who conducted the interview, adds that the campaigns "actually usually don't work." "The second a consumer feels like they're being tricked, they immediately disengage," Nala, an independent dance music artist in Los Angeles, explains. About three weeks after the podcast, Wired called the fanfare around Geese a "psyop," and in the fallout Chaotic Good cleared every client off its website.

First Floor

Betty Hammerschlag Is a Middle-Aged Man

The Guardian and The Quietus both championed Betty Hammerschlag, a lo-fi songwriter often described as a Gen Z Grouper, whose Instagram is all young women and purple-heart emojis. Shawn Reynaldo reports that the project is the work of Tilman Hornig, a German mixed-media artist who is at least 45 and hasn't publicly copped to any of it. The trail runs from a Brussels gig's train ticket, booked in the name of a woman supposedly born in 2004, to an Apple Music credit naming Hornig as the lone songwriter on the 2024 single "Cream." Reynaldo's objection is consent, since Hornig appears to be catfishing fans, promoters and fellow artists. After the piece ran, blush label founder Tim Dalzell posted that the artist, messaging "in the style and dialect of a young girl," once "asked for royalties to be paid earlier so he could buy paints for art school."

The Unbearable Ubiquity of “GG EZ”

Seven seconds of a woman in a gaming chair saying "gg ez," from Kaien Nuen and Angelina "Sugar" Zhang's remake of the song, drew more than 70 million views in the original X post. The song itself came from a mysterious artist called M.Sasuke and set off a dance trend among K- and J-pop idols, whose fans quickly clocked the "tinny glimmer of AI" in its robotic vocals; M.Sasuke soon admitted it was made with AI. The pair got permission and redid it with their own voices. Kieran Press-Reynolds is more taken with what Kaien already does, "humanizing inhuman music" with covers of Brazilian phonk, the slop genre of aura-mogging compilations, on instruments from erhu to suona. Their "GG EZ" takes that a step further, reworking a popular AI song so it's more "ethical" and lifelike.

The New Yorker

Everyone Can Be Migos Now

In a viral A.I. trend, users swap anyone, from their pets to Sam Altman, onto Quavo and Takeoff performing "Hotel Lobby" on A Colors Show, and the stand-ins channel the same aura as the originals. That aura came from originality: Migos revolutionized the triplet flow so thoroughly that pop from Taylor Swift on down adopted the "Migos flow." Brady Brickner-Wood's first thought on seeing the meme was that it was a new form of blackface minstrelsy, a way to inhabit a Black rapper's aesthetics with none of the historical or cultural experience of being Black. Perhaps the clearest precursor, in Brickner-Wood's view, is TikTok, where white teens turned dances created by Black people into frictionless paths to virality, except that face-swapping gives many of its makers plausible deniability. As Brickner-Wood puts their logic, "They aren't the ones pretending to be Quavo; it is their A.I. avatars."

The New York Times

Dolly Parton Was So Fake She Was Real

When a friend told Rhonda Garelick she looked like a drag queen, she was deflated, because it meant she had failed at feigning feminine naturalness. That failure is the hinge of her Times essay on Dolly Parton, since conventional beautification hides the work and drag shows all of it. Garelick places Parton's uberblonde hair and big lashes alongside Mae West and Marilyn Monroe, and argues that Parton was a drag artist of a kind, utterly real about her own unrealness. As Parton put it, "I'm very real where it counts, and that's inside." She never tried to convince anyone the hair or the lashes were hers. The look became one of the great drag personas, and she found that hysterical.
