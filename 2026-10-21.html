The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction
Walter Benjamin · Illuminations (Schocken Books) · 1935

Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction strips a work of art of its "aura," the unique authority it holds by existing in one place at one moment. A photograph of a painting, a pressing of a performance: each copy severs the thing from its origin. Ninety years on, the question he opened (what happens to value when the original becomes infinitely reproducible) only sharpens as the cost of making a convincing copy drops to nearly nothing.

Deezer's detection tool picks out music from models including Suno and Udio, the same Suno that runs Spark. Deezer says the primary purpose of uploading these tracks is fraud, which dilutes the royalties owed to human artists.

You’ve Got to Sell It

At midnight on New Year's Eve 1947, the American Federation of Musicians ordered every union musician in the US to stop recording, in a fight over revenue from recorded music displacing live work. Musicians were fighting a technology for their livelihoods, and Michael Daniels, writing at Jazz Noted, follows what the ban did to Dizzy Gillespie, whose big band went a full year without a studio session. Rough Music

Damon Krukowski, writing at Dada Drummer Almanach, points to a twist in the long fight between artists and capital over music: in the US, generative AI music can't be copyrighted, so "the capital pushing generative AI music cannot own it." For musicians unsure where their own use of machine-learning tools stops and generative AI begins, Krukowski calls copyright "a relatively clear and bright line."
