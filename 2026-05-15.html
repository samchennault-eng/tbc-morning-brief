People vs Algorithms · AI + Content & Editorial Technology
The Great Emancipator
People vs Algorithms delivers a meditation on AI as liberation theology — not the productivity-hack version vendors pitch, but something stranger: the possibility that offloading cognitive labor might free humans to do the thinking machines can't. The piece is careful not to oversell the premise, and that restraint is what makes it land. Anyone who's felt the tug between using AI to move faster and worrying they're training themselves out of the skills that made them valuable will recognize the tension here. The emancipation framing is provocative, but the argument underneath is worth sitting with.
AI and editorial/content quality
The Politics of Dancing · Music & Scene
I feel like a bird with broken wings
The Politics of Dancing runs a conversation with a Palestinian artist tracing the distance between lived experience and how that experience travels — or doesn't — through media. The piece is anchored in a specific tension: Western audiences connect to individual stories but disconnect from collective ones, which is precisely where Palestinian political identity lives. The detour through colonial versions of Islam and childhood under occupation gives the interview real texture. Quieter than a polemic, but the argument underneath it is sharp.
Artist interview
POW MAG · Music & Scene
The Dream Has Left the Building: An Interview with Meaghan Garvey
POW MAG's interview with Meaghan Garvey catches a music critic at the moment of exit — not burnout exactly, but something more structural. Garvey helped define a generation of rap criticism at Pitchfork and beyond, and here she's candid about what the work cost and why she's stepping away. For anyone who's watched the economics of criticism collapse in real time, or wondered what happens to the people who built the voice that platforms then hollowed out, this is essential reading. The dream didn't die; it got acquired and downsized.

02 Music & Scene Music
Jazz Notes
Dinner at the Mangiones'
Jazz Notes uses Chuck Mangione's death as the occasion to recover what the obituaries left out: that his lone Art Blakey album is also the last entry on the discography of Frank Mitchell, a tenor saxophonist murdered in the East River before he turned thirty. Blakey told a critic to watch Mitchell. Jarrett was on the same stage. None of it mattered. The piece is quietly devastating for the way it uses Mitchell's erasure to say something true about how jazz history gets written — around the stars, over everyone else.

03 Music Industry Analysis Music
Music Business Worldwide
What's ByteDance's Soda – and Why Are Tencent Music Investors So Worried About It?
Music Business Worldwide breaks down Soda Music, ByteDance's streaming app that went from quiet beta in 2022 to reportedly 140 million monthly active users — fast enough that Tencent Music's executives used their Q1 2026 earnings call to warn of 'industry chaos.' The piece is useful context for anyone watching how TikTok's parent is moving up the music stack: the same company that already shapes discovery globally is now building the destination. For Western platforms, the competitive playbook here is worth studying before it arrives on their turf.
Your note
Mad Records
Is Live Nation on Life Support?
Michael from Mad Records argues that the wave of tour cancellations and Ticketmaster's monopoly behavior aren't the disease — they're symptoms of a corporate debt story nobody wants to tell. His case: post-2008 near-zero interest rates made acquisition cheaper than competition, and Live Nation built its empire on that logic. Now that cheap money is gone, the whole structure is under pressure. It's a familiar pattern across industries, but the piece applies it usefully to live music.
Your note
Joel Gouveia
If I Ran Spotify
The argument here is structural: AI-generated tracks don't need listeners to damage the royalty pool — they just need to exist in it. A Deezer stat does the heavy lifting: fully AI-generated music is 28% of uploads but only 0.5% of streams, yet it still dilutes per-stream payouts for everyone else. The proposed fix is a simple KYC verification layer at upload. The sharper observation is why Spotify won't move on it — they're a tech company optimizing for shareholders, not a music company with any stake in preserving the catalog's integrity.

Network Notes
The music industry's complex relationship with the charts
Darren's piece takes on the charts as a site of ongoing tension — between what they're supposed to measure and what the industry has turned them into. It's a familiar frustration, but the framing is sharper than most: charts started as a reflection of audience behavior and have gradually become a tool industry players game, inflate, and argue over. The piece is grounded enough to be useful, even if it stops short of a structural diagnosis.

04 AI + Content & Editorial Technology AI
Brad Mehldau Substack
AI and Artists
A working musician making the philosophical case that AI-generated music isn't just aesthetically thin — it's structurally opposed to what art actually does. The argument: art exists to restore meaning when the world changes faster than we can absorb it, which means originality isn't a stylistic preference but a functional requirement. Tools like Suno, framed here as capitalist distraction, invite us to stop searching for meaning altogether. Dense and worth sitting with.
Your note
The Media Copilot
Inside Axios Local's AI expansion
The question isn't whether AI can assist journalism — it's whether it can fix the economics before the patient dies. The Media Copilot podcast digs into how Axios Local is actually running that experiment: 35 cities, one-reporter newsrooms, and specific tools like the "Axiomizer" and "Localizer" handling social publishing and content formatting at scale. Axios COO Allison Murphy is specific about where the line sits on AI in the reporting process, which makes this more useful than most AI-in-media coverage.

404 Media
Software Developers Say AI Is Rotting Their Brains
The vibe-coding bubble may already be deflating from the inside. 404 Media surveys software developers who say heavy reliance on AI coding tools is degrading their own skills — producing hallucinated logic, unstructured code, and a growing inability to verify what they've shipped. One fintech developer quoted here predicts an industry reckoning with the 'everyone can code now' narrative. Worth tracking for anyone using AI to scale content or editorial work: the same dependency patterns show up, just with prose instead of pull requests.

06 AI Product News AI
404 Media
AI Poop Analysis App Offered to Sell Me Access to Its Users' Poops
Jason Koebler's piece is a useful, if viscerally unpleasant, illustration of a structural problem hiding in plain sight: AI health apps are quietly treating user data as a monetizable asset, with or without users realizing it. Koebler traces how a stool-analysis app ended up on a Reddit dataset-trading subreddit, its owner openly pitching access to users' most intimate health data. The pattern — collect biometric data, discover it has market value, sell it — is the same one content and streaming platforms are navigating right now.

Luiza Jarovsky, PhD Newsletter
China's New Agentic AI Framework
The Cyberspace Administration of China just published a dedicated agentic AI framework — making China only the second country to do so, after Singapore's January release. Luiza Jarovsky's Newsletter uses it as a lens to scrutinize what the US and EU haven't done: neither has produced targeted guidance on agentic AI specifically, despite the harms already emerging. Worth a read as a comparative regulatory map, especially if you're tracking how different governance philosophies are shaping where AI products can actually operate.

07 Voice & Culture Culture + News
The New York Review of Books
Pop & Pleasure & Freedom
The New York Review of Books makes a case that good criticism is inseparable from the critic's own taste ecology — what they love, not just what they've studied. The piece draws on Auden's argument that critics should disclose their 'dream of Eden' upfront, and anchors that claim in Ned Rorem's 1968 defense of the Beatles as a high/low provocation. The underlying question — whether pop can be taken seriously without losing what makes it pop — remains unsettled and immediately useful.

Did "Liberal Poptimists" Really Try To Kill Rock? (Not Really.) (But Sort Of.)
Evil Speakers uses Charli XCX's new single "Rock Music" — widely panned, including by fans — as a jumping-off point for a bigger argument: a Jacobin writer's claim that "liberal poptimists tried to kill rock" in the 2010s and failed.
