Discover Weakly
Liz Pelly · The Baffler · 2018

Pelly's 2018 autopsy of Discover Weekly showed that "personalization" was never neutral: the algorithm quietly surfaced far fewer women artists than men, not through malice but through math trained on a skewed catalog. The piece named the central lie of recommendation systems, that they merely reflect taste back to us, and demonstrated instead that they encode existing hierarchies and then scale them to millions of listeners who assume the machine has no agenda.

Firas Shehadeh uses drone sounds because they are what he and Palestinians in Gaza are targeted by; the Western art canon files the same sound under texture. That inversion reorients Depth Map, the noise record he discusses with a closer listen, from aesthetic provocation into what the interview calls forensic listening (improvisations, loops and field recordings doing the work of testimony). Civilians in Gaza, children included, have now lived under that sound for almost three years.

A Closer Listen

Ukrainian Field Notes ~ LVII

Kharkiv has a band called Air Defence System, which is what four years of full-scale invasion does to a scene's vocabulary. Gianmarco Del Re's field notes for A Closer Listen document Ukrainian musicians still recording under intensifying Russian missile strikes, from Kharkiv to the electronic communities of Zaporizhzhia and Lutsk. The strongest thread is his decision to revisit artists he first interviewed when the invasion began, measuring what four years of war has done to how and what they record.

DADA DRUMMER ALMANACH

Artur Nunes, David Zé, and Urbano de Castro: The Lost Stars of Angolan Music

Dada Drummer Almanach marks 27 May 1977, the day the Luanda coup attempt took Artur Nunes, David Zé and Urbano de Castro, three stars of Angolan music. What followed the killings was administrative: records banned, copies destroyed, names scrubbed. Political violence rarely stops at the artist. It goes after the archive, and in Luanda it went after both.

The FADER

Meet Medio Oriente, Chile's wildest post-genre electronic collective

Santiago's Estallido Social riots of 2019 never fully left the streets; part of them moved into the music. The FADER maps Medio Oriente, the label and collective around Segunda Mordida that channels that angst into abrasive digital hardcore (breakbeats, blown-out 808s, hyperpop bleeding into screamo). State violence and surveillance sit in the music because they sat in the founding experience, and the map runs from Segunda Mordida's digital punk outward to a whole roster built the same way.

of songs and poems

The Bittersweet Sound of Iranian Pop and Psychedelia in the 60s and 70s

Nia's essay for of songs and poems opens in a Tehran on the edge of 1979, then rewinds through the pop and psychedelic scene that Mohammad Reza Shah's reforms and the 1963 White Revolution made room for, wider audiences and women onstage included. Googoosh defined the era and then spent twenty-one years in enforced silence; Kourosh Yaghmaei built the Iranian psych that Nas would later sample. It closes as a personal inheritance, an Iranian woman's time capsule of her country's pre-revolution sound.

All Things Jazz

Rest in Peace to the legendary Abdullah Ibrahim

All Things Jazz remembers Abdullah Ibrahim, who died in June at 91, as a musician whose work served as living testimony and an instrument of resistance under apartheid. The career ran eight decades and turned the sounds of Cape Town into a global language, from District Six and the Jazz Epistles to the international run launched by Duke Ellington's endorsement.

The Politics of Dancing

I feel like a bird with broken wings

Western audiences connect to individual stories and disconnect from collective ones, and collective identity is where Palestinian political life resides. The Politics of Dancing sits with Khaled Jarrar, the multidisciplinary artist whose conversation moves through childhood under occupation, colonial versions of Islam, and the way lived experience travels, or doesn't, through media. The broken-winged bird of the title is his own phrase.

Shhh / Peaceful di Giulio Pecci

Cheb Runner «immigration has given rise to Morocco's multifaceted rhythms»

The long view belongs to Cheb Runner. Morocco's rhythmic diversity is an immigration story, the producer argues in Shhh / Peaceful: the country was the final stop for caravans out of Africa and Asia, and the collision of cultures built its layered rhythms. He walks through Marrakech's emerging electronic scene around venues like Kabana, his own archive of street recordings and cassettes, and the diaspora work of Rotterdam's (A)WAKE foundation. What migration put into the music has outlasted every caravan that carried it.
