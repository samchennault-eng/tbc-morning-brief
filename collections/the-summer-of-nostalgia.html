STORYLINE #4

2026, the Summer of Nostalgia

16 articles · Tracking since Jul 2026

The Michael Jackson biopic crossed a billion dollars. Madonna sequeled her own 2005 album into a slot in the World Cup halftime show. Olivia crushed on the Cure. Jay-Z owned Yankee Stadium (and rap dialogue). And throwback jams "Iris," "Mr. Brightside," and "Wonderwall" dominated the charts decades after their release.

It's been a weird summer, and 2026 could be defined not so much by what we created, but by what we remembered.

This storyline assembles the footnotes to the season that scrambled us all. The "returns" come first: the Jigga anniversaries, Confessions II hype machine, the Miles centurion economy, and a Michael biopic that took in a billion. Because everything is structural, and nostalgia more a business model than a mood, we move onto the mechanisms. Catalog acquisition heated up; new music has fallen below a quarter of streaming consumption; and the industry priority is moving money from new songs to old ones. After that, all we're left with are our "feelings" to process: sun stroke nostalgia and calendar dysmorphia.

The desk's AI Remix Era collection sketched the tech half of this story, and this is the cultural side, an account of how it feels to live inside a cultural loop.

The Curious Paradox of Miles at 100

Miles Davis at 100 is as much a business initiative as it is a music memorial. Tom Moon maps the Davis Estate's centennial campaign, run with the labels that issued Davis's primary recordings plus boutique imprints and grey-market opportunists. The roll-out is modeled on classical composer anniversaries: boxed sets, audiophile upgrades, unreleased live albums, tribute tours, and chatty podcasts. The paradox is the man at the center, who lived in tomorrow and rarely looked back. Moon argues that, in a culture where everything fits on the grid, the present needs that radical lunge.

The Intergenerational Duet

Olivia Rodrigo and Robert Smith is a data point, and Chris Dalla Riva has the series. Charting duets between artists at least fifteen years apart, he finds the rate climbing since the Reagan era to a 2010s peak, Sabrina Carpenter and Madonna, Post Malone and Dolly Parton, and Springsteen's young collaborators all on the same curve. His causes are structural: longer lives, recordings that never age out of earshot, a professionalized industry, with hip-hop's collaborative habits and streaming amplifying it all. The Catalog Wave Hit 13. The Money Was Never for New Artists.

Thirteen catalog-buying vehicles stood up in nine weeks this spring, at least $5.4 billion in disclosed capital, and Bruce Ramos reads the wave as a real asset class forming, the way commercial real estate got its buy-side in the REIT era. A worrying trend: old music is still pushing out new music

The numbers behind the vibe. New music fell below 25 percent of streaming consumption in 2025, a first. By January 2026 it held 3.5 percent of Spotify's Global Top 50, down from 9.4 percent in 2024. Benjamin at STVDIO counts four causes: familiarity-favoring algorithms, catalog owners spending to reignite their purchases, the end of a shared mainstream, and a release volume that drives listeners back to what they know.

Ghosts of My Life (excerpt)

The frame for the whole season is twelve years old. In Ghosts of My Life, Mark Fisher borrows Franco Berardi's phrase for what got underway in the 1970s and 80s, the slow cancellation of the future, and describes a culture that lost the ability to grasp its own present, let alone imagine a new one. His sharpest inversion still stings: the young can no longer be assumed to sit at the leading edge of cultural change. Written in 2014, it remains depressingly relevant.
