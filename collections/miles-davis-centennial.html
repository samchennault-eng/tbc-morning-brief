Don't Look Back: Miles Davis at 100

10 articles · Tracking since Jul 2026

In a Juilliard classroom around 1945, a teacher explained to the room that Black people play the blues because they were depressed and had to pick cotton. Miles Davis was nineteen, already holding his own horn. He cut through it clean. "My father's rich. My mama's good looking. Right? And I can play the blues. I've never suffered and don't intend to suffer."

Miles wasn’t about to let anyone define his experience or artistry, then or ever, and that refusal defined him for the next 45 years. He was the avatar of cool who kept detonating the styles he authored, a privileged Black artist who declined every story offered to him about privilege, Blackness, suffering, and art. By the testimony of the people closest to him, he was both a genius and a man who hurt others, particularly the women who loved him. You can’t keep one without the other. And a hundred years on, we look at him and we stare deeply and we see less. Pieces come into vivid focus, but the image blurs and distorts at the edges, even now.

NPR Music May 26, 2026

The Miles Davis century: The definition, and evolution, of cool

Icon and iconoclast, Nate Chinen writes for NPR, both fit Miles Davis, the first of his paradoxes. He looms larger than any jazz musician you can name yet means something different to everyone, because he embodied so many things himself. Chinen traces the cool from the Brooks Brothers hush of Kind of Blue to the funk rupture of Bitches Brew, and reaches for Picasso, one of the few other artists so driven to remake himself. To remember Miles properly, he argues, is to contend with the contradictions.

Nineteen tracks, 1947 to 1970, a way in through the ear. Sunglasses like goggles, a canary-yellow Ferrari, MILES22 vanity plates, a floor-length snakeskin cape. The New York Times Styles desk builds its portrait from the objects that made Miles cool. Pitchfork Jul 8, 2026

Miles '56: The Prestige Recordings

For a few nights in 1956, in a Greenwich Village club called the Café Bohemia, the first great quintet was a working band burning off a contract. Natalie Weiner's Pitchfork review of the centennial box set finds Davis, Coltrane, Red Garland, the twenty-year-old Paul Chambers and Philly Joe Jones cutting four albums in first takes. "We're not trying to prove anything," Davis told a reporter. "All we want to do is blow." Weiner hears jazz's past and future distilled into music that sounds like a place you could live.

All Things Jazz May 20, 2026

Miles Davis and the Fear of Standing Still (1960-1969)

On records like Nefertiti, the horns hold the melody while the rhythm section improvises underneath, the usual order quietly reversed. All Things Jazz catches Davis in the mid-sixties with the Second Great Quintet, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, the teenage Tony Williams and Wayne Shorter, playing standards over something radical. He had assembled the most advanced small group in jazz and stayed dissatisfied, already hearing Betty Mabry's Hendrix and Sly Stone. The restlessness was the point. By the decade's end he no longer believed in permanence at all.

EchoLocator Tom Moon Jul 1, 2026

The Curious Paradox of Miles at 100

The centennial has become a machine. Tom Moon watches the Davis estate and its labels stack boxed sets, sonic upgrades and unreleased live tapes into a monument modeled on how the classical world commemorates dead composers, and finds the joke buried inside it: the man being monumentalized rarely looked back. Davis lived in nextness, Moon writes at EchoLocator, gone before the culture caught up. His proof is the 1964 quintet, five musicians inventing a different music every night, exactly what a monument cannot hold.

Miles Davis at 100: Love for a Complicated Genius

At a Jazz Congress roundtable, the musicians who knew Miles Davis circle the same question: how do you love the music and face the man. Ashley Kahn moderates his nephew Vince Wilburn Jr., Marcus Miller and Terence Blanchard, who remember a man who kept none of his own records at home. Vanity Fair Jul 8, 2026

Wrestling With Miles Davis's Complicated Legacy

Jimmie Briggs first met Miles Davis in 1985, in a Honda scooter ad and a Miami Vice cameo, then spent years holding the genius against harm. His Vanity Fair remembrance sets the litany down without flinching: Chameleon, Bandleader, Maverick, Abuser, Icon. He names the abuse of Davis's wives, points to Pearl Cleage's 1990 "Mad at Miles," and refuses to tuck it under the greatness. He offers an album road map, not a verdict, and admits he may skip the public tributes to play an early record alone.

Tyler King from Jazz Journey Newsletter Tyler King May 27, 2026

Frances Taylor

Frances Taylor gave up her career for Miles Davis. The first Black dancer with the Paris Opera Ballet became the muse of Fran Dance and the face on his covers, and steered him toward the flamenco records behind Sketches of Spain. Tyler King's profile lets her speak from a 2001 interview, and what she remembers is the cost. By the E.S.P. session, she says, "it was only a matter of days before I left running for my life." The centennial can reappraise everything but that.

Nate Chinen from The Gig Nate Chinen May 20, 2026

Miles Davis at Lennie's on the Turnpike

A single photograph of Miles at Lennie's on the Turnpike in 1971, horn bent to the floor, Harmon mute in the bell, half his face in shadow. Nate Chinen tries to pin it down and watches every detail slip. The date is uncertain, the poem he recalls may be misremembered, the bootleg is from another night. Every tribute is a translation, he writes, and Davis refused to translate himself. Chinen lets the mystery stand. We only have what is in front of us, and Miles is already gone.
