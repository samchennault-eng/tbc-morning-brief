The Onset of the AI Remix Era

11 articles · First filed Jul 2026

On May 21, 2026, Spotify and Universal licensed something the industry had spent two years suing to stop: fans paying to remix the catalog. Though seemingly innocuous, it points to an intersection of two powerful currents in the music industry, one that steers how we listen to music, and the other that determines where the money flows.

Platforms optimize for engagement, engagement rewards nostalgia, and consumption tilts toward catalog, which by Luminate's count reached 73.3% of US listening in 2024. And catalog that dependable draws serious, quiet money. Blackstone paid $1.58 billion for the collapsed Hipgnosis fund, saw it revalued at $2.36 billion within ninety days, and sovereign wealth funds and private equity now stand behind Sony's $4 billion grab.

And so, using the most powerful, future-forward technology to push listeners into the past is a no-brainer. Two dissolutions of what it means to be an “artist” follow. Suno's vision dissolves technical skill as a price of entry, while the paid remix tool obliterates the line between creator and consumer altogether. And while the industry fights it out between those two futures, the working artist stands outside, organizing into craft collectives that neither side represents.

Below, we’ll track the story through its phases, and explore the angles through analysis and historical grounding.

Spotify and Universal Music Group Strike Landmark Deal to Let Fans Create AI Covers and Remixes

This is the seed of the story: Spotify and Universal let fans generate AI covers and remixes of participating artists' songs, sold as a paid add-on to Premium and playable by everyone on the platform. The "quiet money" behind $4 billion music catalog deals

Sony spent $4 billion for Justin Bieber, Beyoncé, and Fleetwood Mac in one deal, and Warner took the Chili Peppers' masters for $300 million days later. The cash came from elsewhere. STVDIO follows it to the quiet money behind the catalog boom: sovereign wealth funds and private equity, Singapore's GIC and Bain Capital among them, the kind of capital that measures itself in the hundreds of billions and wants a predictable yield. The frustration writes itself, billions for old songs and nothing new. The counter is colder. That money was never allowed near new artists to begin with.

Billboard Jul 7, 2026 analysis

Is New Music Killing Old Music?

Catalog crossed 69.8% of US album consumption in 2021, Billboard's Robert Levine reported, up from under 40% before streaming took hold. The number reads like old music devouring new, and Levine spends the column complicating it. The biggest catalog acts were Drake, Juice WRLD, and Taylor Swift; Drake alone outstreamed every record made before 1980. Catalog is less a vault of Dylan and Springsteen than last decade's hits aging into the category. Everything eventually becomes catalog.

Spotify and Universal Just Agreed to Let Fans Pay to Remix the Catalog

Run a label, Bruce Ramos writes, and the deal reads in a single line. The catalog stopped being a library people press play on and became raw material fans pay to build on. The industry spent two years trying to sue that behavior out of existence, and now the largest paid platform in music meters it. The fight got productized. Rolling Stone Jun 1, 2026 essay

When the Superfans Start Making the Songs

AI music's real future, Nathan Brackett argues in Rolling Stone, is not fake stars gaming the Billboard charts. It is a woman's panicked texts about hiding from a fling's jealous girlfriend, turned into a song and lip-synced across 28,000 TikToks. That trend pushed Suno to the top of Apple's app store. MiDia's Olivia Jones calls the people doing it consumer creators, and the unsettling part is who they are: superfans who spend the most on tickets and merch, now making their own versions instead of streaming yours.

Suno is paying grants to independent artists… so long as they agree not to criticize Suno

Suno is offering grants to independent artists at the same moment independent artists are suing it over its training data. harpers.org Jan 1, 2025 reported feature

The Ghosts in the Machine

In her now influential essay for Harper’s, Liz Pelly went looking for how Spotify's playlists actually get filled, and the answer she found was ghost artists. Her Harper's investigation, drawn from the book Mood Machine, lays out Perfect Fit Content: the program that seeds playlists with cheap tracks from hundreds of pseudonymous acts, padding the catalog while holding down royalties. Pelly's read is that algorithmic curation, followed to its end, writes the real musician out of the system. The platform filling its own playlists with cheap stand-ins is the same one now selling fans a tool to remix the catalog.

Simon Reynolds Nov 1, 2006 essay

Haunted Audio, a/k/a Society of the Spectral

Simon Reynolds's 2006 Wire essay, the ur-text of hauntology, traces the lineage from dub's ghost captains through the Radiophonic Workshop to Ghost Box's imagined institutions, music assembled from the decaying culture-matter of postwar Britain. Phonography, he writes, never shed its "Edisonian function of keeping the dead alive," and sampling goes further still, "a mixture of séance and time-travel." Twenty years down the line, it hits differently. The industry has licensed the séance.
