STORYLINE #2

The Onset of the AI Remix Era

11 articles · Tracking since Jul 2026

The music industry spent two years suing to stop fans from making AI remixes of its songs. And then, on May 21, it signed the deals to sell them the tool to do just that. Spotify and Universal's new agreement lets fans generate AI covers and remixes of participating artists' catalogs, sold as a paid add-on, with artists sharing the revenue. It's the sort of modest product announcement that gives away the game: the same companies that call AI music an existential threat have decided it's a revenue line, provided the checks clear.

For this edition of Storylines, we'll slowly unravel this story, and try to understand what it means for the larger culture of music. We’ll start with the deal itself, then follow the money that made it inevitable, from the quiet billions behind the catalog boom to the battle lines the platforms are drawing over what counts as real music. We'll meet the superfans who've started making the songs instead of streaming them, and the working artists organizing outside the whole fight. And we'll present it through contemporaneous coverage from some of music's finest minds and best analysis, linked out and annotated below, before reaching back into the archive for the essay that saw all of this coming twenty years ago.

Underneath the deal, there are two currents. Platforms optimize for engagement, engagement rewards nostalgia, and catalog now accounts for nearly three-quarters of US listening. Value that dependable draws serious, quiet money: Blackstone, sovereign wealth funds, Sony's four-billion-dollar money grabs.

Point the most future-forward technology at all that and it pushes listeners into the past. What's left unsettled is the definition of what an “artist” is once tools like Suno and Udio dissolve technical skill as a price of entry and the remix deal completely obliterates the line between creator and consumer. Of course, the working artist stands outside both of these futures, trying to find their footing in a sea of shredded nostalgia. The tool doesn't even have a launch date yet, but the fight over what it means is underway, and we're keeping this file very open.

Spotify and Universal Music Group Strike Landmark Deal to Let Fans Create AI Covers and Remixes

This is the seed of the story: Spotify and Universal let fans generate AI covers and remixes of participating artists' songs, sold as a paid add-on to Premium and playable by everyone on the platform. The "quiet money" behind $4 billion music catalog deals

Sony spent $4 billion for Justin Bieber, Beyoncé, and Fleetwood Mac in one deal, and Warner took the Chili Peppers' masters for $300 million days later. The cash came from elsewhere. STVDIO follows it to the quiet money behind the catalog boom: sovereign wealth funds and private equity, Singapore's GIC and Bain Capital among them, the kind of capital that measures itself in the hundreds of billions and wants a predictable yield. The frustration writes itself, billions for old songs and nothing new. The counter is colder. That money was never allowed near new artists to begin with.

Billboard Robert Levine Apr 15, 2022

Is New Music Killing Old Music?

Catalog crossed 69.8% of US album consumption in 2021, Billboard's Robert Levine reported, up from under 40% before streaming took hold. The number reads like old music devouring new, and Levine spends the column complicating it. The biggest catalog acts were Drake, Juice WRLD, and Taylor Swift; Drake alone outstreamed every record made before 1980. Catalog is less a vault of Dylan and Springsteen than last decade's hits aging into the category. Everything eventually becomes catalog.

Spotify and Universal Just Agreed to Let Fans Pay to Remix the Catalog

Run a label, Bruce Ramos writes, and the deal reads in a single line. The catalog stopped being a library people press play on and became raw material fans pay to build on. AI music's real future, Nathan Brackett argues in Rolling Stone, is not fake stars gaming the Billboard charts. The 300-Person Collective Keeping Lyrics Human

The third position belongs to people like Mike Anning, a Western Australian poet who found that AI tools gave his verse a life he had not imagined, then found the Suno communities around those tools aggressive and mostly noise. Marcus Lawrence, writing in Zinstrel, profiles the roughly 300-person group Anning built in response, The LAMP, or Lyrical AI Music Producers. Its members are not anti-AI; they use the tools seriously and cap the group at 300 on purpose. What they will not hand over is the writing. The lyrics stay human.

Suno is paying grants to independent artists… so long as they agree not to criticize Suno

Suno is offering grants to independent artists at the same moment independent artists are suing it over its training data. From the Archive
harpers.org Liz Pelly Jan 1, 2025

The Ghosts in the Machine

In her now influential essay for Harper’s, Liz Pelly went looking for how Spotify's playlists actually get filled, and the answer she found was ghost artists. Her Harper's investigation, drawn from the book Mood Machine, lays out Perfect Fit Content: the program that seeds playlists with cheap tracks from hundreds of pseudonymous acts, padding the catalog while holding down royalties. Pelly's read is that algorithmic curation, followed to its end, writes the real musician out of the system. The platform filling its own playlists with cheap stand-ins is the same one now selling fans a tool to remix the catalog.

Simon Reynolds Simon Reynolds Nov 1, 2006

Haunted Audio, a/k/a Society of the Spectral

Simon Reynolds's 2006 Wire essay, the ur-text of hauntology, traces the lineage from dub's ghost captains through the Radiophonic Workshop to Ghost Box's imagined institutions, music assembled from the decaying culture-matter of postwar Britain. Phonography, he writes, never shed its "Edisonian function of keeping the dead alive," and sampling goes further still, "a mixture of séance and time-travel." Twenty years down the line, it hits differently. The industry has licensed the séance.
